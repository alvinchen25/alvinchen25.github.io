Its another free day so I mostly work in the morning and Tony and I go for lunch at around noon. We walk to a few restaurants—a buffet that looks yummy but is closed until noon, a few others that seem to just not exist, until we decide to go to Habesha, an beautiful Ethiopian place with lit up pictures on the walls of landmarks in Ethiopia. We order one dish between us, and the waiter tries to get us to get two but I’ve grown accustomed to portion sizes being too big for me here, so I insist on one. It’s the right choice because the plate they bring out is massive, with 7 different sauces to go with the injera.

It comes paired with coffee, which is black and bitter and very warming, and I have three cups in quick succession. The food is good as well and somehow the sauce to injera ratio is just right.

We go to a cafe afterwards, Tugende, and when I’m there I text Arun, who says he was apparently just there hours ago. We are planning to go to Arun’s house at night for an Arunfest, which is some type of bonding activity that he does with the coaches. I don’t think this is the usual name but the coaches call it that.

Tugende is very foreigner-facing and it seems like almost everybody inside is speaking English. I haven’t seen white people in quite a while, and definitely not so many of them, but somehow it makes sense that they congregate in places like this.
The food is expensive and the waiters speak very good English. I get a mango scone and cashew milk, both of which are yummy. I mostly read papers that I need for the semester, and Tony does a geometry problem on his phone before he leaves—he wants to get his swimsuit and his other stuff before going to Arun’s house.

So I work until 4 and then begin to walk to Arun’s house. He tells me it’s a 45 minute walk but google says 1 hr 15 minutes, and I take a wrong turn or two so it becomes almost an hour and a half of hilly walking, and I am sweating by the end but it’s okay because Arun has a pool and the sun is setting so we quickly cool off. Obed is there and we swim! The pool is deep enough that your feet won’t touch the ground if you’re on the deep side, and long enough to practice swimming in if you don’t mind turning a lot. It’s pretty nice to have these hangouts at Arun’s house, and for the Rwandans his pool is the only place in which they’ve swam. They’ve all been practicing and can manage to mostly get from one side to another; apparently this is new, and just a few days ago when the ball in the pool went to the deep end everybody was too scared so nobody went to get it. To someone who doesn’t know how to swim, swimming is remarkable, and they’re even impressed with me (I’m not very good at swimming at all). I teach Obed a bit of backstroke, and he mostly gets the idea. Soon, more people filter in—Innocent, Jessica, Fabiola, and at last Tony and Yuka arrive. They got lost and finding Arun’s house is no easy task. Google Maps doesn’t work as well as you might hope, and oftentimes parallel roads can go for long stretches without having a connection between them, since the roads lie on hills. When Yuka gets in the pool everyone is amazed because she’s actually a swimmer, and she does flipturns and butterfly and shows them some diving.

Eventually we go upstairs and it’s time for the Arunfest to start. It turns out that it’s actually called an Action Learning Set, and I find this a bit of a strange name, but apparently it’s a technique that Arun learned while he was at Oxford, and it’s just sort of to get to know everybody and help people with what they’re thinking about. You start off with a 5 minute meditation session, and then you go around and everybody says how they’re feeling. No particular order. Once that’s done, we set the ground rules for the discussion, which is quick, and then everybody shares a problem they have. It can be anything but it’s good to focus on things that have actionables. We then collectively pick a person and their problem, and the goal of the session is talk it through with that person and help them a bit with their problem. The subject elaborates on the problem, giving lots of details, and then the group asks clarifying questions until they feel like they understand it well enough. Then, the bulk of the session is open questions, where the group asks various questions intended to get the subject to think about the problem in ways that they may not have thought about before.

The entire session is actually quite structured, and Arun is an amazing moderator. During a break we eat South Indian takeaway from a place called Dine Divine. Most Indian restaurants are North Indian, so it’s Arun’s favorite because his family is from the south.

Arun drives u back afterwards—he’s the only person we know that has a car—and we get home by midnight.
