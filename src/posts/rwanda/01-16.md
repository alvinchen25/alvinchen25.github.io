# 1/16

Created: January 16, 2024 11:12 AM

Yuka and I get to AIMS at 8 and print sheets for today. The school I’m visiting is called Hope Haven, and it’s with Alain. I am told in the morning that I’m not speaking to the whole school, just one class. Alain arrives at 8:30 and we take a motobike over to Hope Haven. It’s in the Kigali district but the trip costs 2k because it goes significantly off the road, to a dirt road in a village. The dirt roads are extremely bumpy, with huge erosion streams making it feel like one of those mechanical bulls. We see goats in the village and many kids wave to me and say HI! I wave back. We spend a mile on this road so it feels like we’re going to a really rural area, and I’m expecting the school to probably be older and somewhat dusty, like buildings in the area generally are. Instead, we show up at Hope Haven and it is pristine. It looks honestly quite similar to an American high school, at least at first glance, until you look and see kids doing their laundry in the middle of the school. But compared to yesterday, which was mostly a one-story campus, this place is vast, new, and very large.

We’re taking directly to the principal’s office, and we introduce ourselves to him. He gives us a tour of campus, and it’s even more different from yesterday. He tells us there are around 800 students on this campus. We walk past students holding tablets, and there was no such thing at Byimana—technology was basically banned. I ask the principal and it turns out every student gets a tablet here. Wifi is still controlled by the school, but still to have tablets here is really insane. I think this school has external funding, and it’s one of the private schools in the area.

The principal takes us around to various offices, and we see the IT department, the career development department, the nurse, and many other people. There’s even an American, named Michelle, and apparently Michelle works with financials here for one month every year. We also visit the dormitories, and we see the boys dormitory, which is three stories of rooms surrounded by a courtyard of grass. The boys live 24 to a room in bunk beds, and they each have a locker in the center of the room to call their own. The bathrooms and showers are nice and clean, and the principal says there are 40 showers, which is plenty enough for the students to use when they need it.

Alain asks a bit about the schedule, and apparently the kids all wake up at 5, and do reading at 6, breakfast at 7, and then the school day is 8-5. Boarding school is intense and everybody must follow the same schedule.

The last part of our tour is the kitchen. This is the most surprising—I guess I’ve never seen a kitchen that’s used to feed 1000 people a day. We walk in and there’s a large table entirely filled like a mountain with cut vegetables that stack up a foot and a half tall. There’s a few people around the table cutting more. The next room is for the bread, and there’s another large table with dough, and there’s a lady on the left cutting the dough and throwing it to the right side, where there are others rolling it into a ball and putting it onto baking trays, where they’ll get moved to the oven. There are huge vats where things are boiled and cooked in, and huge ovens as well—everything must be big. The principal tells us the vegetables are grown on campus—they own the land from the school down to the swamp, and they use it for growing crops for the kids. This is such an interesting model and quite amazing. They basically have an entire ecosystem here. And I forgot to mention this isn’t just a secondary school—there’s a neighboring primary school and nursery with the same program. There can be kids that might live their whole lives on this campus. I jokingly ask when their college campus is gonna open, but he says they’ve actually been pushing for that. There are some mothers with babies near the ovens handling vegetables, and the principal says that for mothers that can’t afford the tuition, they can instead work for the school so that their kids can come here.

Then the tour is done and he leads us to the room where we’ll be teaching. There’s an IT guy who comes and helps me set up my computer with the projector—it’s this interesting app that you have to use, and then the board takes up wifi so then i can’t connect to other wifi I think. Later, when I have more issues, such as forgetting to bring a charger, the IT guy helps as well. The staff here seem like a very well oiled machine. When I need to print a set of sheets, the director of studies has me Whatsapp it to him, and in 20 minutes 35 copies of my handout are here, nicely stapled.

Our session with the kids starts a bit before 10:30. We go through the powerpoint but rather slowly, because I’m told they have lunch at 12:30, and two hours is a lot. So we play the 21 game until kids figure it out, which takes maybe 20 minutes. We then also do a chessboard problem and the bridges of konigsberg, which are both quite difficult for them, so we let them work. This group seems to be quite friendly with each other, and for the most part they seem to be actively working so we let them work. There’s a boy who uses his tablet to look up the answers, but it’s okay—It’s good to be resourceful too, I guess (internet literacy is not something many Rwandans are too familiar with). At the end of the presentation we take pictures with the class and some students come up and do selfies with me, which is cute.

After lunch the DOS takes us to a fancy staff room where we eat fancy staff food, with banana paste and cassava leaves and yams and rice. Alain asks me how I like the yams and I say I like them fine and then he says he doesn’t like yams and i’m say ok sure, they are a bit dry.

In the afternoon we’re back with the same students and we give them worksheets because we’ve done too much talking. We tell them to get into groups but make the  teaching mistake of not specifying group size so some are in groups of 8, that just make one student solve the problems. But they still seem to enjoy working together except for one boy who is alone. And he just seems to want to work by himself I guess. 

The math teacher walks us out of the school and we take bicycles back to the main road and bus station. Notably it’s not even a motorbike, since motorbikes don’t really come to this area—it’s really just a guy on a bike who’s in the area. Its 300 for the ride, and its incredibly bumpy once again. The bike riders don’t know english at all and they find it funny when i know the numbers they tell me in Kinyarwanda.

The bike takes us back to the city and then we wait for  the bus back to AIMS, which works out because I have my bus card and the bus is cheap. The ride is fine, and I get bitten by a horde of mosquitoes before we go off for dinner to meet Ari, the program manager of MIT-Africa who has come to visit. Ari is fun and the discussion is good and the food is quite nice as well. He tells about traveling through four countries in Africa over the past week and about the various programs. The restaurant is quite high end and I am situated between the Rwandan coaches talking about relationships and Ari/Arun/Jessica talking about math education. It’s funny and I bounce between the two.
