# January 11, 2024


Today was the first real day of the program! I slept a lot for the first time this trip. I woke up at 9 with our day beginning at 10, so we rushed to AIMS and got started with teacher training.

Here’s how our time here is going to work. On Monday-Thursday we are visiting schools. On Friday we prepare for working with the Math Olympiad students for Saturday, and on Saturday we work with the 24 or so kids in the program. On Sunday we have a Coach’s meeting. We have two weeks, so we visit each school twice.

We’re visiting the schools where there is at least one math olympiad student. Every school visit, we’ll spend part of the day talking to the school administration, part of the day working with that student, and then part of the day talking in a large setting to much of the school.

That last aspect is what we focused on mostly today. Jessica said that we could expect around 300 people in an auditorium, and there would be two of us: one MIT student and one Rwandan coach. The students would be sitting in rows, and we would be in the front. 2 hours were allocated for us to speak to them. Various questions were asked—will we have a microphone, will we have a whiteboard, will we have a projector? The answer was generally, “Assume not.” So it’s pretty much up to our mouths and whatever we can bring to the talk.

Rwandan school is divided into junior and senior, each 6 years. Junior schools are day schools, and senior schools are boarding schools. For each school, we’re likely going to be spending one week speaking to the younger students (S1,S2,S3), and one week speaking to the older students (S456). But Jessica warns us that sometimes the school does what they want and puts everybody in front of us. So the crowds could be as large as 800, which is terrifying.

Jessica runs the workshop and you can tell she’s gotten very good at not just talking in front of people but also teaching people how to talk in front of people. The workshop consists of us three MIT students as well as the local coaches, who are mostly university students.

We go to lunch and they have rolexes—sort of a wrap with an omelette, and it reminds me of some wraps I’ve had in China with egg except with a sort of flatbread outside. During this session, I’m setting next to Etienne and he teaches me numbers in Kinyarwanda. It’s not too complicated and we play a counting game that we’ll show to the kids—it involves two people, and you say a number either 1 or 2 greater than the previous number said. Whoever says 21 wins. By the end of lunch I am almost competent at counting to 21, I think. It starts to rain so we’re stuck at the restaurant for quite a while—we’ll stay until the rain stops. At some point we continue the workshop from the restaurant. Jessica talks about how in Rwanda, rain is a valid excuse for anything—people pass on meetings and kids can skip school because of rain, and this is entirely normal.

When we go to AIMS we see our school assignments and I begin to plan my activities with the coaches I’ll be traveling with. I write up a document here: https://docs.google.com/document/d/1VNPbWpZkimRtou63Tr1XoLQQTz0JuMlL7ilklN79DBo/edit

Obed takes us to dinner at a Nigerian restaurant not too far from our apartment. The lighting is club-like, with blue leds and some spinning party lights. The music is extremely loud and comes from both the restaurant and another nearby building. They play a collection of bangers—I wanna dance with somebody, Mockingbird, some other eminem song, and Billionaire.

We eat barbecue meat on a stick and Jollof rice. The stick is surprisingly thick and the meat is spicy and a bit hard to chew but quite tasty. The rice is as well and I especially enjoy the plantains and the salad that the rice comes with. It’s almost like coleslaw but with beans on it.

We walk back to our apartment and the day is over. We finally get wifi to start working in our apartment (we have to try many different passcodes) but it is still slow and I find I don’t actually need it for much. Up tomorrow at 8 for more planning.
