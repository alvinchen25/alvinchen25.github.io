# 1/8

Created: January 9, 2024 1:16 PM

Just arrived today! It was a pretty insane flight schedule. Leave 8 am EST for a 10 am flight to NYC, get to NYC at noon, wait in the airport until 6:30, fly to Brussels then, (7 hour flight), get to Brussels at around 8:30 in the morning, wait for around 2-3 hours, get on an 8 hour plane to Kigali, Rwanda. The last flight is delayed because of snow in Brussels (halfway through my first movie on that plane I realized the plane had not left).

I feel incredibly grateful and lucky that I got a window seat, because the flight is remarkable. It’s hard to describe how vast the sahara is, how orange too. This terrain went on for hours.

![85AC5D8E-BB19-4BE7-866F-7BE664969229_1_105_c.jpeg](1%208%2004a2ddbceddd40ccb256bb84a23ae4dd/85AC5D8E-BB19-4BE7-866F-7BE664969229_1_105_c.jpeg)

In my travels, I learn of Cracking the Cryptic, which is a youtube channel of two British men solving sudokus with weird and interesting rulesets.

![A1C7E602-E381-46B6-91AE-4E13B7610653_1_105_c.jpeg](1%208%2004a2ddbceddd40ccb256bb84a23ae4dd/A1C7E602-E381-46B6-91AE-4E13B7610653_1_105_c.jpeg)

I arrive in Kigali about an hour behind schedule. We get out and we take a bus to the main airport building. I already paid for an online visa to Rwanda, and then I accidentally get in the line for people who don’t already have a visa, so I paid 50 extra dollars.

I come outside and there’s a whole bunch of people waiting. I think I might be the first person out because I didn’t come with a check-in bag.

I haven’t had wifi or data all this time, and when I finally get Kigali airport wifi, I realize that we’ve really been delayed quite a bit. Theoneste has been waiting for me for almost 2 hours. I think they may not have sent him my flight info and he just got to the airport really early. He tells us that they usually do pickup with the AIMS (African Institute for Mathematical Sciences, from which our program is run) vehicle, but I arrived late at night (about 10 pm) so it was just me and Theoneste. We snapped some pictures together and he found a taxi that could take us to our apartment. The apartment was opened by a caretaker (who we later found out worked at the apartment full-time). We live on the downstairs floor of a larger house. I found this apartment on Airbnb.

Theoneste suggests we go for dinner, and I say sure! We walk about fifteen minutes to a restaurant called Taste Foods and it’s pretty empty—it makes sense though, since it’s almost midnight. I’m surprised it’s open so late but Theoneste says late nights are rather standard for many places here—it just takes longer for the food to come out. We order our food and wait. It takes probably 45 minutes to come, so we get to talk a lot.

Theo is a very funny guy, I can tell immediately. He asks a lot of questions about MIT and is very honest about Rwanda and his life. He is the sixth of 8 siblings and recently started working for AIMS, dealing with the technical side of things and coordinating teachers to run math programs. He shows me a bunch of pictures from the Rwandan Math Olympiad programs, and he shows me the Whatsapp group of over 500 math teachers in Rwanda that he runs. Organizationally, creating a math olympiad system is difficult, and he kind of runs a lot of the technology behind the scenes. This Whatsapp group is how the math competition community in Kigali was built.

![565D63FA-79BE-436A-B5BA-5FBE57FB602F_1_105_c.jpeg](1%208%2004a2ddbceddd40ccb256bb84a23ae4dd/565D63FA-79BE-436A-B5BA-5FBE57FB602F_1_105_c.jpeg)

I also ask him a lot about the logistics of living in Rwanda—should I get a sim card, things like that. He just hands me a router and says use this for now, and you can get a sim card later. I realized I have been quite unprepared since I rushed out of the airport without exchanging cash or getting a sim card.

We both order chicken wings and cokes. Theoneste has two because he loves coke. The wings are tasty, and really anything would have been after so much travel.
