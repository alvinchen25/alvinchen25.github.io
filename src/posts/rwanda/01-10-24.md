# January 10, 2024


We are told that nothing is planned for us today. Tony is yet to arrive and we have time to explore Kigali. We were originally going to have dinner with Jessica but the timing gets fungled. Yuka and I decide that our main plans for the day involve walking around the city and going to the Kigali Genocide Memorial.

We start by walking to a coffee shop, called Question Coffee. This place has many many reviews on google maps, and it’s clearly Western friendly. Jessica recommended it, and you can tell from the vibe it’s quite different from the other places we’ve been to earlier, because those had maybe one google review and no credit card capabilities. Here, they have Croissants and Pain au Chocolates, but in the smaller bakeries there are samosas and little donuts for around 200 RWF. Regardless, the croissant is tasty and the people are nice, and we sit there and plan our day for a bit. We try to plan the rest of the day, and we decide to mostly go off of a list that Jess sends us on WhatsApp.

Walking around is interesting in its own right. Google maps is relatively reliable and takes us through these small walking paths that go between the main avenues. They’re narrow but fun and sometimes you really have to watch your step.

![12536AAD-D79B-46E0-A84A-80C2363FB2A1_1_102_o.jpeg](1%2010%2096f048e6109a4047994c22519c593a5a/12536AAD-D79B-46E0-A84A-80C2363FB2A1_1_102_o.jpeg)

After coffee, we decide to walk by the convention center. This is considered one of the newer and richer parts of town, and you can certainly tell. The roads are spotless and there’s less people just walking around the area. I thought the convention center would be an open mall-type thing, but nope—it turns out it is just for conventions and closed to the public! They are building a mall next to it though, but we won’t see it. A lot of big construction projects are going on—the big stadium near where we live, where football matches are held, is also in construction and will open next year.

![6F0E7ECD-C7CA-41B4-8C9F-752C3E5E1BAE_1_102_o.jpeg](1%2010%2096f048e6109a4047994c22519c593a5a/6F0E7ECD-C7CA-41B4-8C9F-752C3E5E1BAE_1_102_o.jpeg)

Yuka and I are just past the convention center where we see white people, so we decide to talk to them and their tour guide and ask them how to get a bus to the genocide memorial. It’s probably 5 miles away, so it’s not really realistic to walk all the way. The tour guide says just take a moto-taxi it’s safe I promise. The locals clearly all take moto-taxis, and we could tell Hirwa was a bit disappointed when we said we didn’t want to take one yesterday. We ask Jessica because she’s a foreigner too, and she says she can’t advise using it because MIT would get annoyed and we’re definitely not supposed to. But Jessica also said she uses them all the time. This is a huge green light for me and so we decide hey, let’s take the moto taxi to the genocide memorial. The tour guide told us the ride should be at most 1500 so we flag down two taxis (they are everywhere on the streets) negotiate down to 1500, and by how easy it is I can tell this is definitely more than the ride should be. Off we go, and Yuka and I are both hanging on to our drivers shoulders while sitting behind them. I’m also squeezing my thighs into my driver, and I can sort of tell I’m not supposed to do this, but there’s not much else to hang on from and I feel a bit unstable. I’m extremely tense and it feels different from any way I had traveled. You get so close to other bikes, to pedestrians, to cars. The reason motorbikes are the fastest form of transportation is that they’re somehow immune to car traffic. There’s a motorbike line in between two car lines, and drivers will just weave through everybody—it really felt like a race. My driver goes quite fast and brakes quite hard, and I’m guessing this is standard—he wants to complete the trip quickly. And quick it is—what probably would have been an hour-long commute by bus is over in 10 minutes. I pay 2000 because he wanted 2000 in the beginning and I figure why not for getting there safe on my first mototaxi ride ever.

![51EE2EC5-003B-4907-8CA0-E5B9C33C6A82_1_105_c.jpeg](1%2010%2096f048e6109a4047994c22519c593a5a/51EE2EC5-003B-4907-8CA0-E5B9C33C6A82_1_105_c.jpeg)

The genocide memorial itself is well organized and well kept, and it’s clear it’s well funded and it feels like many foreigners go there when they visit Kigali. It’s brutal and heartwrenching, especially because it’s so recent. And because there are pictures and there is information about many details in the process. And because there are still so many survivors who live the consequences today. Kigali is a young city, both as a whole and in terms of its people. But you get the sense that nobody has forgotten. There is such a stress around safety, and a pride when it comes to safety. There are safety checkpoints where my bag is searched multiple times—I believe I went through three today.

![C375E402-733F-4BBF-AAC5-61F3FF8F275E_1_102_o.jpeg](1%2010%2096f048e6109a4047994c22519c593a5a/C375E402-733F-4BBF-AAC5-61F3FF8F275E_1_102_o.jpeg)

Afterwards, we walk past a small park where a bunch of locals are chilling, and Obed finds us and together we walk downtown. It takes a bit less than an hour, mostly uphill in the humid afternoon.

Obed tells us a bit more about his experiences—he was part of the first cohort of the Rwandan math olympiad. Many people involved in the past few years, especially those that have done well, have gone overseas. Quite a few have gone to MIT, some to the UK, some to India. But Obed has still been here in Rwanda, which is how he became the head coach. He’s still actually a university student, but the university is not too demanding, while running Rwanda Math Olympiad certainly is, so he spends a lot of time with the program. He tells us he wants to do a good job with things this year, and then he’ll likely try to move out of Rwanda later on. He wants to see snow (all seasons are roughly the same temperature in Rwanda).

![EED6C85B-8BAE-450E-A77E-60991CDFBF9A_1_102_o.jpeg](1%2010%2096f048e6109a4047994c22519c593a5a/EED6C85B-8BAE-450E-A77E-60991CDFBF9A_1_102_o.jpeg)

We go relatively fancy for lunch (is it lunch? it’s 3 pm). We walk into the hotel and it seems like one of the ones just for White People—we walk in and everybody is a foreigner, of course. This is the first time I’ve experienced air conditioning in Rwanda. I honestly would not be surprised if it’s also the last. Our restaurant is on the rooftop of the hotel, and it’s cool with great views. The prices at the restaurant are near american, so the Chicken Tikka that I get is around 15000. I get a smoothie as well and we talk more about traffic and roads and traveling.

Afterwards, we go to get the bus cards. Obed doesn’t use buses so he’s getting a bus card for the first time as well. Buses are cheap but very packed, at least the ones we saw were. We want to go to Kimironko market and get fruits, and we go to the bus stop in the station, and there’s a huge line, so we decide to take the mototaxi instead. This time, it actually does cost around 1500, and it’s about a 20 minute ride all the way across town. Yuka’s driver apparently asks her things like is she married, where is she from, what does she do, things like that. My driver is silent.

We pay and walk into the market and go to the fruits section. This here is a competition as well. So many people have mangoes and we just want a few. I start talking to one merchant and he says NI HAO MA. It turns out that he actually knows quite a bit more than that—he can do very basic conversation, and it’s clear he sees Chinese people quite a bit. And then other merchants crowd around, and it turns out that they know some Chinese too! I guess there are many Chinese people that come around here, and it turns out that there is even a chinese supermarket here, across from the Kimironko market. I am probably serving as quite good entertainment for these merchants, but Obed seems a bit concerned and thinks they are being too much—he tells us we can ask them to leave. But we just get two mangoes and walk away.

At this point Tony is about to arrive from the airport and we’re assigned to pick him up, so then we have to get to the airport quickly. Whoops, I guess we have to take the mototaxi again. This time we use some really small route with tons of bumps and up-and-down hills. They call Rwanda the land of ten-thousand hills. We go through residential areas It looks like an area where many people live and there are not so many cars, just motorbikes. When we hit the first reverse speed bump, i feel myself slipping from the seat. This is somehow scarier than the first ride.

But Tony is picked up, all is well, Obed leaves us, and the day is mostly over. Tony and I go to Taste Foods, which is 24/7 apparently, and we get their african food (since Theoneste and I got western food when we went, and these were separate pages on the menu). There is so much, and it is delicious. I get the Lowombo Beef, which gives me rice, cassava, sweet potato, and a curry-like sauce with beef. And it feels natural and delicious. I eat too much, and we buy some Rwandan beer and go home (I turned 21 on my flight to Rwanda, so I had bought Rwandan beer before I had ever bought American beer).
