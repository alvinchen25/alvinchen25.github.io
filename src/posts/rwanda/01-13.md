# 1/13

Created: January 13, 2024 9:56 AM

Today was all teaching. I’ve discovered that you need to be loud to teach in Rwanda.

We get up at around 7 and get to AIMS at 7:40. Class starts at 8:30 so we prepare and figure out how to print in the time before then. Maybe it’s because I have a mac, so I cannot print on my own computer, so I always have to borrow computers to print our handouts. I get the sense that maybe they think we are using too much paper, because we keep on running out of paper and having to go find more. I think it’s a worthwhile investment though because a good set of problems in front of you can go a long way, and the only way the students can keep the problems is if we print them out.

I meet Salvi, who’s the Rwandan student that got admitted to MIT this year. He’s committed, and we talk about MIT for a bit. This program has sent a few students to MIT every year for the past few years. Some of the students I’ll work with today will likely be there in a few years. I find the program and it’s success and outreach remarkable—to have consistent MIT admissions over many years for a small country like Rwanda is amazing. And the rapid success of the progrm is also amazing—a far higher percentage of Rwandan youth know about math competitions as compared to American youth, no doubt. I had to ask teachers to set up math competitions at my elementary and middle schools, but here almost every student hears of it from their school, because of the outreach that the program has done (Theoneste and his Whatsapp group!).

The students trickle in—12 in the senior group and 6 in the junior group. I do some introductions, where we say our names and then a fun fact about ourselves. I tell them I can hand-whistle, and then demonstrate. Tony says he sees kangaroos because he’s Australian. Yuka says something else. The kids stand up in turn very formally, say their names and tell us they’re pleased to be here, and then they sit down. I have brought a bag of Werther’s original candies, and I give each student one. We separate into groups. I’m not teaching the first period so I get to bounce around between Yuka and Tony’s sessions.

Not to critique their teaching, because teaching in such a new environment is really really hard, but I can tell both of them getting somewhat flustered at times. You can write something on the board, and say “does that make sense?” and get blank stares, no response. We were used to the coaches like Etienne and Innocent being quite talkative and easy to work with, but these students are clearly much more nervous themselves, and it’s difficult to communicate. Sometimes, you ask a student to come up to the board and prove something to the whole class, but the student takes really long in the proof, and is facing the board the entire time. And it’s a bit tough to interrupt them, but also you can see the other students getting bored, so things can get a bit awkward. I get to thinking a bit about how I want to run my session.

I leave for a bit and find Arun. He’s the guy who started all of this, along with Jessica. He’s from New Zealand and was on the IMO team there, and came to Rwanda around 5 years ago. He says he worked as a trader for a few years and then moved here to teach computer science at African Leadership University. He says he doesn’t know why they let him become a faculty member—he didn’t do so much computer science before. He has his own company now, and it’s been going for about 4 years. Arun is definitely on the wealthier side of people living here—he has a company, and he apparently lives in the posh part of town near downtown. Obed told us the other day about how Arun has a pool, and many of the coaches go swim at Arun’s pool sometimes. That’s how they learned to swim.

I ask Arun a bit about starting up the program here. They’ve grown so much over the past few years. Last year, they hosted the PAMO (Pan-African Math Olympiad). In 2022, there had been 9 countries at the PAME. This past year, there were 31. How did you do it, I asked. Hard work, and Jane Street funding, he says. 

We have a break after the first period with tea. We arrive late but have some African tea and bread. It’s good. Arun and I sit next to the littlest kid. We ask him how the session this morning was. He said it was too easy. I tell him I have some harder questions for him coming up.

I go back to the classroom and it’s now my turn to teach the juniors. There are 6 of them and I learn their names. I give them the handout I put together yesterday, and we begin. I describe ideas such as gcd, relative prime-ness, and the Euclidean algorithm. Immediately, I see that one student is a good amount more advanced than the others. His name is Prince, and he seems a bit bored. That’s okay though, I don’t plan to talk for long, and after that he can work on problems at his own pace. I talk for maybe 10 minutes, prove that consecutive Fibonacci numbers are relatively prime, and then set them off to work. I tell them they should work with their partner, and it’s nice because we have three groups of two. I think this mostly works well, although the littlest kid and the student next to him are reluctant partners. It’s okay though, I think they warm up to each other. I introduce modular arithmetic, and eventually they begin working towards Fermat’s little theorem. By the end, they have mostly gotten to a proof of the theorem, which is remarkable for kids who have just been introduced to modular arithmetic—these students are fast! I rush through a proof just in time for the end of class at 12:30, but then Yuka comes in and tells me lunch is late and I have 20 more minutes. This is okay though, they don’t mind working through more of the problems and seem to have enough fun with the problems. Salvi and Obed sit in on my session and I can tell Obed is pretty happy with my teaching.

We go off to lunch in decently high spirits. The students come along, and Yuka deliberately sits a table away from Obed and I because she wants to sit next to students. The students sit in the other room. Yuka moves back to our table. The seats around us are soon filled with coaches. I try cassava leaves at lunch for the first time. There’s also fried fish, and everything is yummy. Food here has just a lot of bare ingredients on the plate, but because of that it feels so much more natural than food anywhere else. Cassava leaves are an amazing vegetable.

After lunch I go and teach the seniors. I realize my first example is not a good example, and I rush through it, but the students ask me questions about it. I kind of black box it and tell them it just works, but I feel a little bad for not doing a full example or choosing something more reasonable (I thought a real proof needed real analysis, but I might have just been wrong). The second example was much better, and after the initial hiccup the session goes smoothly. I find that the students here are quite capable and have seen quite a lot of problems, and they all diligently work for the hour that we have. I walk around and everybody has made good progress. Near the end it starts raining crazy hard, thick thick droplets that create a din that makes it hard to hear anybody. I wrap up the session and I’m basically yelling as I tell them about how I love invariants.

Yuka comes in and that means the intense math is over—it’s time for our activity. We get them into groups of 4, with at least one junior per group. We get two coaches to volunteer and they happily do. We give them the activity, where each team gets three sheets—one is a crossword grid, containing blanks where they will put digits. One pair gets the down clues and one pair gets the across clues. And so they need to work together to finish the puzzle.

The concept of the puzzle is a bit difficult to explain to the students—I don’t think they’ve really seen crosswords before, so communicating that one digit per answer and the structure of the crossword takes some time. So we give them about 50 minutes to do it. By the end, everybody’s locked in and I feel bad when we have to close things up. I ask them to write their names on the sheet and they do, and then they reluctantly surrender their sheets to us. The winning team gets a prize, once again candy that I bought from the US. Werther’s Original!

Later on we rest at the apartment for a bit. We go out to eat dinner somewhere later and we run into Jessica of all people on the street. Jessica joins us for dinner, but deciding where to eat is still difficult. Yuka wants to eat African food, specifically Rwandan food, and Jess wants to go to Asian Kitchen LOL. Yuka is clearly very against Asian food—we all know Asian food well enough. Jessica has probably eaten enough Rwandan food, and she says other food is better! But in the end we go for Rwandan. So we find a nearby restaurant and it seems reasonable, and we sit down. When Jessica comes we all ask the waiter about what they have. It turns out this restaurant doesn’t have anything. Maybe 10% of their menu is available. In the end, I ask if the restaurant has any meat (which is almost all of their menu). She says no. Yuka agrees to leave, but she still is anti Asian Kitchen. We find another African place and this time they do have food. I order chicken curry, Tony gets chicken as well, and Yuka has a soup with tripe or skin or something in it. It’s interesting, and we get to talk to Jess about her experience here in Rwanda and how everything ended up happening. It’s refreshing talking to somebody who has outside perspective and inside perspective. She says she came a year to work for AIMS, and then just ended up staying. I ask her if she plans to be here long-term, and she’s not sure—she has a 2 year old and the Rwandan school systems are not great, so it might be hard to stay once her kid grows up. But who knows. She really helps us understand some of the differences culturally—she tells us the kids that we taught today were only informed on Thursday that this camp was happening. Some came from 3 hours away, waking up at 5 am, and they were told about in 4 days ago. Jess leaves right after we finish our food, saying that her kid needs to go to sleep.
