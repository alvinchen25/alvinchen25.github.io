# 1/15

Created: January 15, 2024 1:58 PM

Today I learned so much about Rwandan schools.

Yuka and I get to AIMS at around 8. The AIMS van will drive us to our schools—Nepo and I to Byimani, and then Etienne and Yuka to Nyanza. Jules comes along and goes to Nyanza as well.

The car ride there is around 2.5 hours and we get to see the Rwandan countryside for the first time. Rwanda is a remarkably dense place, because there are maybe 10 million people living in rural areas, so on the drive you just see lots of farmland and houses everywhere. Driving is impressive because we’re on a road with one lane each way, and you constantly have to pass slower moving bikes, carts, trucks, and people. In the city, the motorbikes will crowd right next to you. The Rwandans point out the crops as we pass them—lots and lots of sugar cane and banana trees and rice paddies and maize. I make it a point to try eating sugar cane the way they do, ripping off the outer pieces with your teeth and chewing the flesh.

I learn that Nepo and Etienne are actually younger than me. Nepo is 20 and Etienne turns 20 this week. All the coaches recently finished high school, and it’s incredible that this program, which runs math competitions for the whole country, is mostly organized by people my age or younger. They’re thrust into positions of such responsibility because they’re ambitious and there’s really so much to do. The oldest coach other than Obed is Innocent and he’s probably 22 or 23.

We also review counting and I’m pretty comfortable counting to 100 now. I will speak Kinyarwanda to the kids today!

We arrive at Byimani and wait outside the gate. We’re welcomed in by the Director of Studies, or the DoS. He kind of runs the academics at the place. The campus is quite pretty, with many plants everywhere. But we walk from one side to the other in maybe 4 minutes, and he tells us 900 students live here—it is very dense. He gives us a mini tour and shows us to the little classroom where the students will be. There’s around 7 chairs, 3 tables, and a blackboard but no chalk. Later, the math teacher has some students bring in chalk and a new chalkboard. But in the meantime, we push the tables together, and it’s just me, Nepo, and the four AIMS kids doing math together. It takes me a while to connect to wifi and I need Nepo to hotspot me, but in the meantime I give them the geometry problem that we showed the coaches yesterday, which I somehow remember. One kid solves it quickly and I nudge the other kids a bit, reminding them about power of a point. Later, it all comes together and I talk a bit more again about using wishful thinking ideas in problem solving. I also give them the problem where you try to prove that any integer that is relatively prime with 10 divides 1111…111 (length k) for some integer k. This is a surprisingly hard problem and I talk a bit about the Euler Totient Theorem, and then there’s a student who comes up with a super simple combinatorial solution that I haven’t thought of at all. We don’t have too much time before lunch so we just talk about their experience at this school and doing math, and the students are really fun. They’re all in their final year at this school, and it’s clear they’re a bit more comfortable with themselves now. But it’s also clear they’re a bit over this place. Some of them have been to the IMO. They’ve seen much of the world, and they’re ready for it.

Then it’s time for lunch and the math teacher takes us to the teacher room. We eat lunch with the staff. We get rice and this white paste made of maize. Nepo tells me the name but I sort of forget. You then cover it with beans, cooked in a soup with a little bit of some other vegetable. Many teachers are walking around with mountains of rice on their plates. At AIMS, in the city, we have some amount of variation, and the food changes a bit, but here at Byimani, the food is not so different from day to day—we’re in the countryside. Nepo and I introduce ourselves to the staff briefly, but I’m more excited to go back and do math with the students.

![1F8D4ABB-C4B6-4085-AF57-AE5F2F1820E0_1_105_c.jpeg](1%2015%208caf479b5372492aa01a246d6e3b8071/1F8D4ABB-C4B6-4085-AF57-AE5F2F1820E0_1_105_c.jpeg)

We come back to the small classroom with the AIMS kids after lunch, and I ask them about their lunch, which was predictably the same as ours. To eat this food everyday would definitely get tiring, and remembering how many people complained about my high school’s dining hall I feel a bit bad. They tell me that yes, the food is like this almost every day for lunch and dinner, and breakfast consists of porridge and a bit of bread. Pretty much everything is carbs. On sundays they either get two eggs or some meat. To them the AIMS food is incredible—tea and meat and vegetables and an egg and some watermelon, and it’s only here that I realize how fortunate AIMS is and how fortunate the RMO is to be able to run through AIMS. We do another problem after lunch—thankfully Tony has found a nice set and we have both a combinatorics and a geometry problem that we work through in the hour after lunch as well. I think overall the problems are a bit hard but they are dedicated. I want them to talk to each other a bit more but they seem to mostly like working alone first, which is understable. While talking about the geometry problem I show them geogebra, even though I know they probably won’t be able to use it soon since they have very limited computer use.

Then it’s time to talk to more students. They take us to the big hall and surprise, we have a working projector and microphones too. This is about as good of a setup as we can expect and the session goes quite well. We have somewhere from 60 to 100 students, and many of them have brought notebooks and are taking notes. I think they have fun with our activities, and most of what we wanted to include works for them. We did have a video which didn’t really work because we connected to the projector but not sound. But otherwise things go smoothly. Nepo is an excellent teacher, and he knows what the kids do and don’t understand. Oftentimes I say something and Nepo says it again in Kinyarwanda for the younger kids. We take selfies with the students. It starts raining but we wait outside for a bit and eventually we can go home.

On the car ride home it gets dark but I ask Nepo a bit about his time in China and we talk about industry and traveling and whatnot. Rwanda is smaller than Massachusetts but getting from Kigali to a province can take 6 hours, because the countryside is hilly, the roads are not straight, and the speed limit is very low. In America when you see hills you just construct a tunnel through them because going fast and straight is the priority.

We spend a lot of time talking about schools, because it turns out all of us went to boarding school, for different amounts of time. Boarding school in Rwanda is quite intense, and oftentimes they’re in rural areas so there really isn’t anywhere to go. Students have three trimesters separated by about 2-3 weeks each, maybe a bit longer. During the trimesters, the students do not leave—there really is not much else to do. So the small campus we visited is all a student sees for almost 90% of their time from ages 12-18. And this is at Byimana, a prestigious school which I imagine is much bigger than many other schools. The IMO team from the previous year had multiple students from Byimana.

The age gap in schools is huge because they go for 6 years, and Nepo mentions that there used to be a lot of bullying. A senior 5 student could, for example, take a senior one student and get them to wash their clothes. Kids in secondary school need to learn to wash and dry their own clothes before leaving home, and so there are sometimes these issues of bullying or of stealing clothes from others. Especially since they are all in uniform, and they might just have 2 copies of their uniform, a student may need to wash clothes 2 to 3 times a week. Other bullying included beating if kids didn’t do what older kids told them, and other punishments. Boarding school at 12 means it can be difficult to stand up for yourself. But now, Nepo says, bullying risks expulsion so it doesn’t happen too much anymore.

Phones and computers are generally disallowed and apparently if they suspect you have a phone they might evacuate everybody and search the rooms, checking under mattresses and whatnot. Kids often get quite creative though and sometimes people cut out sections of books to put their phones in. Schools may sometimes have a computer lab that kids can use sometimes, but otherwise they’re mostly cut off from the internet, at least their own usage of it.

I ask Etienne about how much people make here—a schoolteacher makes about 300-400k RWF a month, a banker maybe 700k, somebody at a more advanced place maybe 1000k or so. Of course living costs are way different. I think about how we’ve been going to restaurants and spending 7k RWF (5 dollars ish) per person and thinking this is a good price. But the average Rwandan does not go to restaurants, it becomes clear.

Jules wants to show us the view of the city, so we take an alternative route as we come back to AIMS. It’s through one of the tallest hills in the area, and so the people here call in “Norway”. It’s a great nickname, and Etienne tells us about how much Norway has developed in the past few years. After Norway you go through some of the richest parts of town, and you see all the fancy hotels clustered together—four points, sheraton, and whatnot. I imagine a large amount of the white tourists that I saw at the airport will be at places like these. I look inside four points and see a gym, which I doubt you would find in most places in Kigali.
