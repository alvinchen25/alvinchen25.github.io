# 1/9

Created: January 9, 2024 1:21 PM

I wake up to a text from someone named Hirwa that he is going to pick me up at around 9:30 to 10, which he sent at around 4 am. I had assumed that I wouldn’t need to do much today, since the other two students, Yuka and Tony, had not yet arrived in Rwanda. Somehow through, I’m awake at 10:07, as if my body anticipated this, even though I had slept at 3 am the night before and had basically slept only 3-4 hours on the plane ride. I tell Hirwa he can come anytime. He arrives by motorcycle at around 11, and I find him outside the gate, and greet him. He tells me that he’s a coach for the Math Olympiad team. He chastises me lightly for not checking that he is indeed Hirwa—“what if i am a scammer”—my bad! He asks me if I am okay with motorbiking and I am a bit hesitant. He says that some westerner he knew from the past didn’t like motorbiking, but it made it a lot more difficult to get around, since motorbiking is what they use. I’m not sure if that’s what Jessica (westerner who helps run the program—she has a two year old, and we’ll meet her soon) uses, but hey, I think I can trust Hirwa on this? During an orientation session for MIT Africa a few months prior they basically said don’t use them. The number one cause for death for travelers is motor vehicle accidents, and motorbikes are about as unsafe as it gets. I think a little and decide maybe it is a bad idea. I’ll try my best to not use them and go for buses instead, but I might change my mind at some point. In the end I tell Hirwa let’s walk for now. He looks a bit disappointed.

We walk to AIMS, and on the way we get stop for some breakfast. It’s around 11:30, and we go to a bakery on the way. Hirwa gets a pizza (looks rather different from pizzas here, it’s thick and has sauce and many vegetables on top) and I get a samosa and a donut (not donut shaped as americans would conceive it, but rather a large donut hole). We share African tea. It’s interesting because it’s tea mixed with a lot of milk, and some spices (Hirwa thinks its ginger?). He was trying to tell me the word for it in English, so he put the Kinyarwandan word into google translate, but it returned virus instead of ginger for some reason. Not the best! The tea comes with sugar, and it tastes awesome. The pastries are a bit dry but they go well with the tea, and I’m happy.

We spend a good chunk of the morning educating me on how payment works. When we finish our meal, it comes out to 5800 RWF, and Hirwa walks up to the counter, enters the phone code, and pays. I’ll need to learn to do that soon, but I haven’t set up my phone payment system yet.

We get to AIMS and walk into the lobby. There’s a basketball court in the courtyard, a large dorm facing the front, and a bunch of smaller buildings behind it. AIMS is the African Institute of Mathematical Sciences. I learn that AIMS is a Masters program, and it’s the host of the Rwandan Mathematics Olympiad team.

Sitting in the lobby is Theoneste and Jules, who’s been coordinating a lot of our program. Later, other people trickle in—Obed, the head coach, and Jessica. I’ll say a bit about each of these people

Jules—Jules is very well dressed, with a nice button up and glasses. He looks like a business man. Jules works for AIMS and organizes a lot of the activities and logistics of what we’re doing. You can tell he’s one of the oldest people there.

Obed—Obed comes in wearing a vaguely familiar shirt and a flannel on the outside. Somebody mentions Jane street and then I realize the shirt he’s wearing is from Jane Street—LOL. You go to Rwanda and Jane Street is still there (Theoneste in particular is very excited about Jane Street). Obed says he got the shirts from PAMO (pan-african mathematics olympiad). I can tell he’s one of the most traveled people here, because he has gone to many countries, including Morocco for PAMO and Oslo for IMO. You can tell he’s really ambitious, and I think he’s applying to business programs. 

Jessica—Jessica is from Ireland and she moved here in 2018. She says she spends about 70% of her time here in Rwanda. She has a two year old, and Jules and Obed have met him, and apparently the kid likes Jules more. The others joke about the visa payment for “little Ireland” aka Jess’ house, and she says the payment is chocolate.

Together, one thing I noticed right away—and think is awesome—is how they love to tell funny lies.

There’s an interesting thing that happens in the conversation though, and it’s about Burundi. Theoneste jokes that Jules is from Burundi, which turns out to be not true, but they talk a bit about how Rwanda is a lot more developed, especially in the Kigali area. I don’t entirely remember why this comes up, but I think the cleanness of Kigali and its developments has been a source of pride for many in the country. They say it’s the cleanest and safest city in Africa. Obed says it is not so developed, however. But he has traveled more. But despite what the others say, he tells us Kigali is not so developed. 

We head to the dining hall, which has an outdoor and indoor section. Inside, there are trays of food, much like catered meals back home. There’s salad, some pasta, rice, sweet potato, some meat, some sauce, and some other stuff I’m forgetting. I’m surprised at how much I like the rice. It’s around 1, which means Hirwa and I had eaten maybe one hour ago, so I’m not too hungry, but I make sure to get a bit of everything. Hirwa stacks a plate full of food, and I am doubtful he can finish it—the pizza he ate earlier was not exactly small. But it turns out that Hirwa has a huge stomach and though he eats somewhat slowly the food is slowly cleared. Jules jokes that in Rwanda there are 7 volcanoes, and one of them is Hirwa’s plate.

After lunch, we go back to the lobby, and there’s a professor holding office hours, and it turns out he is from Rochester, and he’s teaching at AIMS for a few weeks. He finds out that Theoneste and Hirwa do computer science, and he’s very impressed, and jokes about how much money they’ll have. We all take pictures together, sitting in front of a banner that says AIMS.

While taking the pictures, Yuka, another one of the MIT students, texts that she has arrived, and is waiting outside. Theoneste and I go to the airport to pick her up. It turns out that there is a good amount of traffic, but AIMS is close to the airport and we soon arrive, taking the AIMS car. An interesting thing about going to the airport is that you have to go through security even if you’re just picking somebody up—they search the car and my bag. I find out that I have been to the airport as many times as Theoneste (him picking me up was his first time there, despite it being just a few miles from AIMS), and he leads us in the wrong direction, but I somehow remember my way from last night and then we find Yuka.

We head back to the apartment in the AIMS car, and Yuka drops off our stuff, and we go along with the AIMS car back to AIMS. Many people have left already, so it is just Yuka, Theoneste, Hirwa, and I. We learn that either Theoneste or Hirwa has been googling Yuka, because they thought she interned at facebook, but it turns out it was a post on facebook about her interning somewhere else. But it was so funny—”you mean there is another Yuka Machino with a gold medal at the IMO who interned at Facebook?” Yuka shows them her resume and we see Theoneste’s Linkedin, which is quite impressive. He’s put his info together quite well, and he seems proud—I’m very happy for him.

Yuka and I decide we need SIM cards, and Hirwa takes us to go get sim cards. We decide to take a taxi (no motorbikes once again), and so we walk to the taxi place and are driven to a sim card place. Its clear that taxis are much more expensive that motorbikes. We successfully get sim cards and load money into them, however, we can only put in our cash into the account for now, and I didn’t pick up any cash from the airport. Later, I figure out a website called heptapay that allows me to load my SIM card from my credit card. It doesn’t work at the time, and I also try Remitly, but sadly this fails to work. I find out that it’s because my phone somehow logged into Remitly in the UK, and Remitly customer service also doesn’t know why things aren’t working. Eventually things are figured out —I have an eSim card for data, and I have a physical sim card for paying and other things. I probably could have gone without the eSim card but hey too late.

Yuka needs shoes since she forgot to bring enough shoes, so we go to a shoe store and buy shoes. Shoe stores are quite different in Rwanda—there’s about 20 merchants standing outside the store trying to get us to go in and maybe 20 more sitting inside the store. Once Yuka starts trying on shoes there’s 10 people throwing shoes at her and trying to talk to her. The starting price for the shoe is 38k RFC (rwandan francs, 1k rfc is around 0.75 dollars). Yuka asks Hirwa to negotiate for her. He talks to the merchant for a few minutes and the price comes down to 23k. She pays. I can tell from the way Hirwa looked that it probably could have been negotiated down a lot more, but we are foreigners and they can tell we do need the shoes. 25k is the most expensive shoes can be normally, so this is already paying a lot. And we should probably pay more than locals anyway. Hirwa mentions sometimes when bargaining, if he wants to know the true price, he’ll just leave. You know you’ve gotten close to the minimum price of an item when they actually let you leave.

We walk back to the apartment. We stop by KFC. It’s a lot more expensive than other places, but it looks interesting inside. We don’t get anything. We stop by some stores. And then we find a milk bar. It just serves milk—I’m in love. I get a mug of warm milk for 500 RFC and it’s one of the best things I’ve had in a long time. I don’t think I’ve quite had milk like this before. It’s stored in these large hot containers, and we eat it with some pastries on the side, which are kept in plastic containers at the back of the shop. I also get a banana, which is smaller than the bananas we have in the US. When we pay in the end, nobody has been keeping track of what we ate. We just count what we ate ourselves and individually pay them. Things are a lot more trust-based.

After that, we walk home, and our journeys for the day are over. Kigali is really beautiful at night.

Oh and I don’t think we have hot water in showers?
