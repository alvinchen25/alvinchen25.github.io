Many of you know that I am likely moving to Hong Kong next year, and I visited for a week in July to scope things out.

When I arrive in hong kong there is a typhoon, level 10. It means it's the worst storm in two years, hurricane-force winds, stores and restaurants closed—it means stay inside. I sit in my hotel room scrolling reels and eating snacks until the directive relaxes, and then I decide to go climbing, and to my surprise there are lots of people on the streets and the subway and the climbing gym. I start talking to a few people at the gym and they joke about grandpas in the neighborhood still having dimsum together during the worst of the storm. Eventually you get old enough to dgaf about typhoons.

I read about the history of hong kong and how it was kind of passed around like a child caught in a centuries-long custody battle. For most of its colonial history, it was a place where nobody fully belonged, until it slowly carved out its own identity. And as I walk through and around the city, I find that, strangely, i do feel some sense of belonging, that this is a place I could learn to live in. I learn that HKers drink lemon tea in the summer, and do sports like stream hiking and coasteering. They buy produce at fresh markets and play badminton in the park. There's a clear language barrier, but I also have a language barrier with my own grandparents. I'm working on it all!

The city captures something of an immigrant experience, of different identities jammed together and lots of things that don't make sense. The buildings don't really make sense (hong kong is the most three-dimensional city) and I hear people code switching between canto and english and mandarin and that mostly doesn't make sense either. But there's something real about the confusion and in figuring things out, and I've decided that I do think the city makes sense for me.

One of the guys at the climbing gym grew up in Virginia, and he comes to hong kong to see family every summer. He plans to fully move here next year, to write, teach, and maybe work with bookstores. Later, we go on a hike on his side of the island, and we see ocean and mountains in one direction and skyscrapers in the other.

'how long will you stay,' i ask. he tells me he doesn't know, that people here don't think so far into the future. you can't, really, with the unrelenting change that happens in a city like this. you take life as it comes.
