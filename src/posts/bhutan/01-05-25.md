# January 5, 2025


I wake up at 8 after sleeping a much needed 10 hours. Breakfast at the hotel is at 8:30 and I am a bit surprised when the first things they bring out are corn flakes and coco pops. Seeing cereal here is funny and it turns out that coco pops with hot milk is pretty tasty. There are clementines and watermelons, as well as some porridge. There’s also pancakes and apple juice, so the meal feels curiously American.

After breakfast we text Dorji and Sangay, and he says they’ll come pick us up after lunch.

We spend the morning preparing materials, putting together the diagnostic test we’ll give students in the beginning, as well as a collection of fun problems without too many prerequisites that we’ll use for an early session. 

We’ve had a few meals at the hotel so we decide to go out and find some different food for lunch. The one downside of this hotel is that it isn’t really located in a town, but rather on the highway between towns, so the nearest restaurants are all a bit of a walk away. I find a restaurant, one of the closer ones, but it’s around a 5 kilometer walk mostly uphill, so we get packed for the day. My watch says I’m about 20% adjusted to the altitude, and we take frequent breaks since we’re all out of breath. 

![E4C8D778-F7F2-414F-8898-66C441C43357_1_102_o.jpeg](bhutan_E4C8D778-F7F2-414F-8898-66C441C43357_1_102_o.jpeg)

At one of these breaks we see a store on the side of the road and we decide to go in and get sunscreen—the sun is very strong here, and it contributes to the large temperature differences between night and day. Serena and Zian both came out for the walk with winter coats (it’s probably mid 50 degrees F in our rooms), but i’m in just a shirt. In the sun, it feels like over 70. While in the store, there’s a Bhutanese man there. He’s got some toilet paper and a large bottle of oil, and he keeps on looking over at us and smiling, like he finds us somehow funny. We end up talking a bit, and we ask him for restaurant recommendations in the nearby area (the place I found had 2 google reviews—people definitely don’t use Google maps much here or go to restaurants too often). He tells us he actually runs a restaurant, wanna come? And so we get in his car and our walking situation as well as our need for food are resolved all at once. His restaurant is at the end of a small dirt road, a place we’d never think to look, but the seating room is wonderful and has a view of a valley and a temple on a hill. It also has a large wooden penis (more on this later). 

At the restaurant we get momos—dumplings, as well as spinach and pork with rice. We aren’t really familiar with Bhutanese restaurants at this point, and it turns out that ordering each of these gives really small portions—you’re really meant to order quite a lot and share. But we realize this too late and it takes a long time for food to come so we just don’t order too much. The momos are really delicious though!

![96691CFA-0F55-4AE9-9108-289BD7E199EC_1_105_c.jpeg](bhutan_96691CFA-0F55-4AE9-9108-289BD7E199EC_1_105_c.jpeg)

So we leave the restaurant and right in the neighborhood there is a penis store and it seems to specialize in making penis carvings, drawings, and other phallic art. It turns out that this area is famous for fertility reasons, and the temple on a hill that we saw is a temple that couples will specifically go to and pray at if they have fertility issues. The phallus also symbolizes strength, and Dorji tells us later it’s very common to have some sort of phallic imagery in traditional buddhist households here.

![0DF6B954-4565-4AFB-AC50-13A08A566472_1_102_o.jpeg](bhutan_0DF6B954-4565-4AFB-AC50-13A08A566472_1_102_o.jpeg)

Dorji and Sangay pick us up. They’re taking us to some tourist sites nearby. We pass by the Punakha Dzong, a large monastery which around 400 years old. It seems like each district has a Dzong and it’s an important center of religion and culture. Punakha was the old capital of Bhutan, before it moved to Thimphu.

We then go to a trail where we climb a small hill to get to another temple. The trail has some steeper uphill sections but it’s not too long. We see many stray dogs on the way, and Dorji tells us about how most of the dogs in Bhutan are stray. There used to be a lot more stray dogs, but the government instituted a program to sterilize the dogs in the country two or three years ago, and you see noticeably fewer dogs now. Dorji says he’s afraid of dogs, but then it turns out that he has a dog. The dog he has is a really small one though, one of the little cute ones.

![AD5A8486-92CB-4AE3-AAEC-A131C6767B1D_1_102_o.jpeg](bhutan_fa395bc4-7336-4e91-a35c-f259a76032ff.png)

Dorji tells us about how he went to South Korea last month, and about all of the differences that he felt there. He was there for a conference—Korea is funding smart classroom initiatives. He tells us about how he saw traffic lights for the first time, how he thought the streets were so clean and modern. We ask him if he liked the food, he said no, they gave me octopus on the first day. I don’t think octopus is eaten in Bhutan, it’s landlocked.

We then go to the Punakha suspension bridge, the longest bridge in Bhutan. It’s 180 meters and you can see the river beneath your feet. It’s a nice area and we take some pictures.

![A26139FC-AFF0-4400-B9B9-74852DE8F241_1_105_c.jpeg](bhutan_A26139FC-AFF0-4400-B9B9-74852DE8F241_1_105_c.jpeg)

Our last stop is a small town called Khuruthang, and we stop and just make a loop around central town area. There’s a few karaoke places, shops, and one restaurant called KFC, for Khuruthang fried chicken. We pass by a few stores selling alcohol, and we ask Dorji what alcohol he drinks. Dorji tells us about how he likes to watch movies with ashtray in one hand and drink in the other. Or he’ll go to bars with his friends and drink, or at karaoke. Smoking is not too common here—Dorji is apparently the only ministry person we’ll meet that smokes. But drinking here is traditional, and we see bhutanese whiskey and beer brands.

We go home and I rest a bit before dinner, which is white rice with chicken and broccoli, as well as some pumpkin soup and a fruit cocktail. It’s simpler today, which I like, and the macros are there too. Tomorrow we’ll go to TTI, where the camp is held, and meet the students and teachers!
