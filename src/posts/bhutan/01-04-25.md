# January 3, 2025


My flights:

RDU-BOS

BOS-ICN (seoul)

ICN-BKK (bangkok)

BKK-PBH (paro, bhutan)

From the time I leave my house in RDU to the time I arrive at our hotel in Wangdue Phodrang, Bhutan, it’s almost exactly 48 hours!

It doesn’t really make sense to me that boston to seoul should take 15 hours, because new york to hong kong was also about 15 hours, and it feels like that one should be longer since hong kong is much further south.

I stare at the flight map at various points and realize the reason the flight takes so long is because the natural shortest path takes you through the arctic circle, but also through much of Russia and North Korea.

In Seoul, I find Serena, one of the other two MIT students on this program.

The Bangkok-Paro flight is booked separately, so we need to leave the terminal, get our checked bags, and then wait to check back into the terminal to go to Paro. The issue is our flight arrives in Bangkok before 1 am and doesn’t leave until 7:30, and so check in won’t start until after 4:30. My hopes of going into one of the terminal lounges and maybe showering or resting in the lounge for a few hours are very quickly dashed.

After going through customs, Serena and I find Zian, the third member of our group, and we have some 2 am Thai food which is surprisingly delicious, even though we were the only people in the restaurant. I get a bowl of noodle soup and we share some calamari, and the noodles are spicy but excellent. It’s training for Bhutan where we are told the food is very spicy. We talk a bit about the program and competition math—Zian is a freshman and is much more familiar with the current scene.

![IMG_4638.jpeg](bhutan_IMG_4638.jpeg)

We eat a bit and wait until 4:30, when check in is set to begin. There’s already a lot of luggage in front of us waiting to be checked, and it turns out that there’s lots of people going to Bhutan. Two families have large TVs, and Zian and I try to guess what’s happening—if you’re Bhutanese and you want a large flat screen TV, do you need to simply bring in on an airplane yourself?

Once through security, I find that inside the terminal is much nicer than outside. All of the food looks exciting, so I get mango sticky rice at one place (comes with a mango and 3 colors of sticky rice (white, purple, and green!! I can’t really figure out what the green flavor is), thai iced tea (I get some special with three toppings but with the ice and the toppings there is sadly not so much tea), and a grilled chicken dish.

The flight from Thailand to Paro is good but it finally gets to the point where I feel like I’d be tired enough to sleep, and a sleep for 30 minutes, but then food comes, breakfast, so I eat that, and in the latter half of the flight there are beautiful views of the Himalayas so I don’t sleep then either.

![00DA9A20-0331-4158-956E-E397E6C2E9DB_1_105_c.jpeg](bhutan_00DA9A20-0331-4158-956E-E397E6C2E9DB_1_105_c.jpeg)

I brushed my teeth at Bangkok which is helpful, but I still feel nasty and disgusting—my shirt has gone sweaty and then dried multiple times in this transit alone.

The Himalayas feel so staggeringly close, and not really so tall. There’s one that feels much taller than the others, and I get really excited—it is Everest, what else could it be—and then I look at the map and realize we are not close enough, no, this is something else. And as we descend the plane moves so close to the valley I can see individual trees up close.

The airport is beautiful, and unlike the metal skeletons characteristic of many of the world’s airports, Bhutan’s has a wooden, more homely vibe—the building itself isn’t by design so different from other buildings, it seems, and it’s covered in art.

We deplane, exchange some money and get sim cards, and go outside, where a man introduces himself as Sangay. He has he is our driver, and he’ll take us to our hotel.

Sangay tells us that it’s about a 3 hr drive to get to our hotel—it looks close on a map, but the speed limit is 50 km/h, and he works for the ministry, so he really cannot be speeding. The roads are winding and it seems like it wouldn’t really be possible to go much faster. Sangay passes other cars quite often as is.

We stop for lunch at what looks like a monastery attached to a restaurant, and there we get ema datshi, which is a peppers dish with cheese, as long as lots of other tasty foods. The ema datshi is unique and interesting, and I find that mixing it with the light purple rice gives a really strong flavor to the rice.

![0868A41C-5904-42B2-975C-A6D2BFEBC7F7_1_105_c.jpeg](bhutan_0868A41C-5904-42B2-975C-A6D2BFEBC7F7_1_105_c.jpeg)

We make another stop where we see a monument, built after the war Bhutan had in 2003, Operation All Clear. Apparently rebel groups from India were settling in south Bhutan, and there was a military operation to remove them. The monument features 108 chorten built to remember that war. They teach us that in Bhutan, you walk around things clockwise for respect, so we take one clockwise loop around the monument. Going clockwise happens when driving as well, since in order to go around a roundabout clockwise, cars must drive on the left side of the road. So we wonder if this is the reason that cars drive on the left here, or if it’s because of the connection to India and British colonization.

![8657F884-F39E-45EB-B782-6F24C890FED5_1_105_c.jpeg](bhutan_8657F884-F39E-45EB-B782-6F24C890FED5_1_105_c.jpeg)

We find out that we’re not going to be staying with the students. It seems like they wanted to give us better accomodations, so we’re instead at a hotel around 10km from the camp. This is both good and bad—it’s nice to have a room to myself, but also we think it would be great to really live at camp and spend more time with the students.

In order to get to our rooms, we go down two floors—since buildings are built on valleys, they often conceal many many stories. The rooms face the river.

The concierge at the hotel hands us three keys, and we randomly choose rooms. I look inside my room and I’m very happy—I have 2 twin beds, nice cozy chairs, a balcony, a mirror, so what’s not to like? I then go see Zian’s room and it’s about double the size of mine, with multiple tables, three beds, a bathtub—it could fit a family of five. I’m slightly disappointed for a moment, mostly because if I were in Zian’s room it would be much easier for me to do yoga, but really that’s the only downside of the room (until I find out my hot water doesn’t really work but Zian’s is almost instant). I feel a bit spoiled for being jealous!

We decide to do most of our work in Zian’s room, so we prepare a bit for first day activities on Monday, and then we go eat dinner. We find that breakfast and dinners are provided with the hotel, and we’ll eat lunch with the students. We talk about whether it would be possible to go a bit earlier and get breakfast with students sometimes, but we’re not sure. There are many unanswered questions about what to expect, so I guess we won’t know until we see it.

We eat dinner at the hotel and there’s a chicken cooked in dark brown sauce which goes really well with the rice. The people working at the hotel are incredibly nice and they bring lots of things for us to try, such as a fruit cocktail which has watermelon and a mysterious fruit that we can’t figure out, until we ask and they tell us it’s just pineapple, but somehow it tastes really different from the pineapple we’re familiar with?!

Dorji greets us—he is one of the people organising the program and I think he’s living at the camp with the students. He says we have a free day tomorrow to get adjusted and visit some places in Bhutan, and then we start on Monday!
