In the morning I find a small trail and I decide to follow it up for a bit. It's not very well cleared and some parts are a bit hard to maneuver and covered with thick vegetation. It's also very steep. I walk for about 15 minutes but I reach a point where it's hard to continue without scrambling. I look back and the sunrise is incredible.

Breakfast is plentiful but I am tired. We talk about things other than math today—I think we’ve thought so much about math and math education and these students that it’s a bit refreshing to talk more about our lives back home. 

In the morning I run test review. The test had gone okay but we realized there were some pretty crucial misunderstandings with both algebra and just proof techniques. I really like test review though, it's a bit easier than teaching new content, and I think the students are a little bit more invested, since they want to see how they did and what they might have messed up on.

I also made a handout just for algebra practice, since we agreed that working on more fundamental things was important. I think it's also important for confidence reasons to do easier problems. There's a common philosophy in the world of math about selecting really hard problems, and how competitions like the Putnam have a median score of 1, but really we want to have balance. So the problems here range from factoring and expanding to solving quadratics, things you would expect to cover in high school, but also things that are crucially important. For the most part we get the impression that the students know how to do the problems, but a lot of times they know the method but not really the intuition.

I like the style of classes where I don't need to lecture too much, so I can sort of let students work on problems in groups and I can just walk around and help people. Working one-on-one is still the most effective way of learning, since you can really see what someone is understanding.

![alt text](78CAD974-8369-4410-B7EA-B90B9E029274_1_105_c.jpeg)

Later on Zian and Serena teach a class on methods of proof, since we have also realized some students will make logical mistakes in the setup of a proof, for example when considering the contrapositive or the negative of various statements. So much of math problems is being able to understand the problem, and especially for students who don't have English as their first language, I think we probably should have stressed this more early on in the program.

I think the reduction in difficulty here is actually been really helpful and the students seem to be happier and hopefully learning more.
