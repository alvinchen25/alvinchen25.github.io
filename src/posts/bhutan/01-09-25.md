# January 9, 2025


I don’t know if this means the days are long or short but I’ve found it harder than normal to remember what happens in a day.

I wake up at around 5, despite taking melatonin last night. I never have a problem falling asleep, I just wake up too early and can’t go back to sleep.

We have eggs and fruit and chicken sausage in the morning. When we had eggs earlier in the week, they were really runny (whites are transparent), but today they’re just how I like them 😀.

![E8A05134-020B-4C5D-8E72-3FE0DA4A368A_1_105_c.jpeg](bhutan_E8A05134-020B-4C5D-8E72-3FE0DA4A368A_1_105_c.jpeg)

On the drive to camp in the morning, Sangay asks us if they’ve been changing our bedsheets. We say no, but we’re totally okay with it. It’s alright and I’d rather not have somebody have to come in and clean every day. But Sangay says that in Bhutan usually at hotels they’ll change bedsheets every day. I tell him really, it’s okay, but he’s made up his mind that he’ll talk to the hotel manager.

He tells us there are some really nice hotels that tourists stay it sometimes. One is called Six Senses, and it’s apparently over 1400 dollars a night (for context, the median annual household income in Bhutan is just under 5000 dollars). This number feels absurd and I don’t believe it, but I look it up later and it’s true. It’s hard for me to understand that the hotels could be so much more expensive than the ones in NYC, but I guess the only people that come to Bhutan are very well off, and it’s hard to keep and maintain really luxury facilities in Bhutan. I look up the Marriott prices and they’re also around 600-700 USD/night. The hotel I’m at has a lot of local Bhutanese people—for example, many business people on trips from other places in the country will stay at the hotel, and we’ve only seen two other groups of foreigners.

In the morning I’m not teaching so I have some free time. It actually works out nicely because I need to take a shit in the morning. It was my first time needing to shit during school hours, so I tour the various bathrooms before deciding where to go. There’s a ‘staff’ bathroom and a student bathroom, but it’s not super formal for this camp, so a bunch of the students use the staff bathroom. But still most of them go downstairs to the student bathroom--there isn’t an agreed-upon norm. The staff bathroom is definitely a bit cleaner, but the main thing I notice is that the toilets have no toilet paper, just bidets. And this is okay, I’ve used a bidet before, but usually I’ve also used some toilet paper to dry off, but here I spray my ass with water and the water is dripping off of me. The options are either to stay there until I sort of dry off or leave and get my pants wet, so I sit on the toilet and watch instagram reels for a while. When traveling, it's important to bring paper.

I put together a divisibility handout for tomorrow, and then I start looking for problems that would work for this week’s test.

After a while, I finish most of the preparations I need and I decide I can go for a run. My watch says I’m about 67% adjusted to the elevation, and so I’m prepared to run very slowly, but I haven’t done cardio in quite a while and I feel like I should. I run along the main road, which is mostly fine, except there are some cars where the gas smells really bad, and I have to hold my breath while I’m running. There are no sidewalks and I don’t really think people here generally run on the roads too much (but they hike and play sports like soccer or archery, the national sport). I run two miles, and in the middle I find this nice sandy beach area on a river bend, and I stay there for a bit. The water is cool but not too cold, and you can tell it flows from the mountains.

The running is indeed difficult, and though I’m going at a leisurely pace I can feel my heart pumping through my chest. My watch tells me that my VO2 max has decreased since coming here. My heart rate stays high for a long time—-even an hour after I've finished running, it hasn’t returned to its resting tempo. It’s good practice though and I think I’ll keep on trying to run on our breaks.

![7A21EFA8-B6AC-452D-A49D-2C49D9DB0416_1_105_c.jpeg](bhutan_7A21EFA8-B6AC-452D-A49D-2C49D9DB0416_1_105_c.jpeg)

One of the girls is singing Eenie Meenie by justin bieber and sean kingston at lunch. I talk to a few students about music and they tell me Ed Sheeran is coming to Bhutan this month. He’s the first ever big western artist to come to Bhutan, and everyone knows about it. It's crazy that it coincides with our one month visit here. Unfortunately the kids will be at camp when he visits, and I will too. I would kind of like to go but I don’t know how feasible it is.

In the afternoon I teach areas, and I think it goes pretty well. The students are clearly very new to area concepts, but having pictures to draw and deal with is always nice. I spend some time going over the ideas behind scaling areas—if two triangles are similar and the side length of one triangle is twice the other, then what’s the ratio of the areas? Almost all of the students said 2 initially, so we spent some time talking about how it’s actually 4, and what this says about dimensionality. 

During one of the breaks, I show them sporcle quizzes, so we name countries in Africa, Asia, Europe, etc. We also do US states but only one kid really knows US states. Some students know a lot about geography, and especially in Asia they know almost all the countries. Definitely way better than the average American kid. It's also particularly impressive for a second language. I actually learned that for many students they know four languages. Those in the south know Hindi, a Nepalese language, English, and Dzongkha, the national language.

I also show them zetamac, which is a bit of a party trick for the kids.

I’m seriously trying to memorize their names now, and we go through names together a few times in class. Hearing me try to pronounce their names is funny for them, I think.

On our drive back to the hotel, Sangay tells us that if we ever want to go anywhere after school, like a store or into town, we should just let him know and he’ll take us. It's very nice of him, and I honestly didn’t think about this possibility. I say next week we’ll maybe do that. I do want to see more of the country beyond our hotel and the school. I’m looking forward to having a more adjusted sleep schedule where I can actually do things after school without falling asleep.

As expected, the hotel manager is there when we get back and she tells us we can change our sheets if we want, and it seems like Sangay has spoken to her. It’s clear that the Ministry people are really trying to make sure we’re treated well. But the hotel staff is all incredibly nice and I don’t have complaints.

I have the brilliant idea of running a trivia night for the students and I get to preparing it.

Dinner has stir fried buckwheat noodles with pork strips, and it’s delicious. The only complaint I have with the food is that the salads often come mixed with mayonnaise or are otherwise extremely creamy, but I’m used to eating raw vegetables just raw, without sauce. The cream makes the vegetables heavy when I’m kind of looking for a lighter thing. But of course that’s just me being picky. We finish almost all of the food today.
